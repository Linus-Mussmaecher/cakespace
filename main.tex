The adventurous quest for forbidden knowledge about the properties of the cake space has begun.

\section{Preliminaries}
What is the space of cakes, anyway? Is it a topological space? Which topology should we consider?

When baking a cake, we may consider the ingredients given by the recipe as a vector of real numbers.
When extending each recipe by the ingredients it doesn't contain, this places all cake recipes in a common vector space of some (currently unknown) dimension $r$.
This allows us to apply the methods of linear algebra to cakes.
In the large vector space of possible recipes, not all vectors must correspond to a mixture that can be turned into a tasty cake by an appropriate sequence of instructions.
We call the subset $\cakespace \subseteq \field{R}_+^r$ of recipes that do \emph{cake space}.
The cake space is not necessarily a space, yet it is closed under positive scalar multiplication.

\section{Operations}
Baking a recipe for a larger or smaller number of people than originally intended naturally leads to (positive) scalar multiplication.
When baking a cake for consumption by a celiac, it may be wise to first apply the operation given by the matrix
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
to the subspace spanned by $\{\text{four}, \text{gluten-free flour}\}$.
However, the addition of cake recipes is a yet underexplored topic. 
Clearly, a baker may choose to add or remove a quantity of a certain loved or disdained ingredient $i$ (such as sugar or raisins) to a recipe, corresponding to the operation
\begin{equation*}
	r' = r + \lambda e_{i} \qquad \lambda \geq - r_i\text{.}
\end{equation*}
This may not result in a tasty cake for all $\lambda$ or $i$.
Similarly, one may wonder if the addition of two valid cakes $r,s \in \cakespace$ is necessarily contained in cake space again.
This leads us to the question of convexity.
